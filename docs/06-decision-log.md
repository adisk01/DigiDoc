# 06 — Decision Log

Every significant call, the alternative I rejected, and why. **If a reviewer only reads
one document, this should be it.**

---

### D1 — Client type: primary care clinic network, not a consumer platform

**Rejected:** consumer telemedicine app; hospital group; pharmacy chain; insurer.

**Why:** the consumer front door is won — Halodoc holds roughly 40% of the digital
outpatient segment with a full-stack ecosystem. A fifth entrant adds nothing. A clinic
network under **capitation** has the cleanest economics: clinician minutes convert
directly to margin without any volume assumption. A PE sponsor can also replicate the
system across add-on acquisitions, which is the multiple-expansion story.

**Would change if:** the client already owned consumer distribution, in which case the
provider-side tooling becomes a margin play on existing volume instead.

---

### D2 — Doctor-supervised, not autonomous

**Rejected:** fully autonomous patient-facing diagnosis.

**Why:** three independent reasons, any one sufficient.
*Regulatory* — keeps the system inside the existing frame for medical practice rather
than requiring a new one, which matters given that UU 17/2023 and PP 28/2024 recognise
telemedicine without fully prescribing operational standards.
*Clinical* — the system structurally cannot examine, measure vitals, or observe over
time.
*Commercial* — no Indonesian provider will accept the liability, so an autonomous product
does not sell regardless of its quality.

**This is the load-bearing decision.** Every other choice in the design follows from it.

---

### D3 — Red flags in deterministic code, not in the prompt

**Rejected:** prompting the model to identify emergencies.

**Why:** auditable, testable in CI, reproducible, and impossible to prompt-inject past.
The model may add escalations; it may never remove one. When a regulator or a coroner
asks how an emergency was handled, the answer needs to be a line of code, not a
temperature setting.

---

### D4 — Retrieval over Indonesian guidelines, not model memory

**Rejected:** relying on the base model's medical knowledge; fine-tuning on medical text.

**Why:** guidelines change and must be versioned; every clinical claim needs a citable
source for the reviewing doctor; and local guidance (PNPK, Fornas) is thinly represented
in any base model's training data. Fine-tuning would additionally destroy traceability —
you cannot cite a weight.

---

### D5 — Text-first, not video, not voice

**Rejected:** video consultation; ambient voice scribe as the v1.

**Why:** connectivity outside Java is unreliable and data cost matters. More importantly,
Bahasa Indonesia ASR quality on regional accents and Indonesian-English code-switching is
an **unmeasured risk**, and I will not build a product on top of an unmeasured
dependency. Measure word error rate on real clinic audio first, then decide.

---

### D6 — Postgres + pgvector, single container

**Rejected:** dedicated vector database; microservices; Kubernetes.

**Why:** at pilot scale a separate vector store buys nothing and costs operational
surface. Infrastructure sprawl in a two-day prototype signals poor judgement, not
sophistication.

---

### D7 — Hard age gate at 2 years

**Rejected:** treating paediatrics as a normal path with adjusted thresholds.

**Why:** infants cannot report symptoms, deteriorate quickly, and present non-specifically
— a septic neonate may look like "sleepy, not feeding." The asymmetry of harm is extreme.
A hard gate is worth the lost coverage.

---

### D8 — Mental health: detect, do not assess

**Rejected:** including mental health triage in scope.

**Why:** risk assessment depends on rapport, tone, and hesitation — precisely what
asynchronous text discards. The failure mode is catastrophic and irreversible. Detection
plus immediate human handoff is the only defensible design.

---

### D9 — Buy the model, build the safety layer

**Rejected:** training a model; buying an off-the-shelf clinical AI product.

**Why:** no defensibility in the base model and no chance of matching frontier quality.
No off-the-shelf product handles Indonesian guidelines, Fornas, or INA-CBG. **Buy the
intelligence, rent the plumbing, build the parts nobody can sell you.**

---

### D10 — Ship the eval harness as a first-class deliverable

**Rejected:** demoing the chatbot alone.

**Why:** in clinical AI the eval *is* the product. It is also the clearest available
signal of engineering seniority, because it demonstrates knowing what could go wrong
rather than only what works.

---

### D11 — Report small-n results honestly rather than inflating the set

**Rejected:** generating hundreds of synthetic vignettes to make the denominator look
respectable.

**Why:** synthetic vignettes generated by the same model family being tested measure
self-consistency, not correctness. **A dishonest denominator is worse than a small one.**
"100% on 15 cases, which is not enough cases" is a stronger statement than "97% on 500
cases" that a reviewer cannot trust.

---

### D12 — Write kill criteria before writing code

**Rejected:** defining success metrics only.

**Why:** a project with no pre-agreed stopping condition cannot be stopped once it has
organisational momentum. Deciding in advance, in writing, is the only time the decision
is made honestly.

---

---

### D13 — Hard post-operative gate at 30 days

**Rejected:** treating post-op complaints as ordinary primary care presentations.

**Why:** this is the most deceptive case in the whole system. Wound pain and low-grade
fever read as routine, and the intake will classify them that way — but anastomotic leak,
surgical site infection and DVT present exactly like that, with a completely different
threshold for concern. **The base rate moves underneath the model and the model does not
know.** Recent surgery becomes a mandatory intake field and a hard routing gate.

---

### D14 — Surgical and procedural care: recognise and route, never assess

**Rejected:** one blanket "no procedures" line covering everything.

**Why:** surgical decisions are exam-and-imaging decisions, and a confident text-based
answer there is fabricated rather than merely wrong. Oncology, trauma, dental and
ophthalmology each need their own boundary. The system's job at the surgical edge is
recognition and routing, then stop.

---

### D15 — Every failure degrades toward more human involvement, never less

**Rejected:** graceful degradation that keeps drafting during partial outages.

**Why:** model outage, latency spike and empty retrieval all resolve to the same
behaviour — intake open, drafting off, cases to the human queue. **There must be no
failure path where the system acts more autonomously.** Stated as an invariant so it
cannot be eroded by a later performance optimisation.

---

### D16 — Safety-netting issued at intake, before review

**Rejected:** issuing all patient guidance after the doctor signs.

**Why:** async means a queue, and the illness does not pause while the case waits. The
patient gets plain-language "if any of these happen, go now, don't wait for a reply"
instructions immediately, and can re-trigger the safety gate at any time. Time-since-intake
is an input to triage, not just a metric.

---

### D17 — No untracked orders

**Rejected:** generating investigation recommendations without follow-up infrastructure.

**Why:** orphaned results are a leading category in real clinical negligence claims and
the thing prototypes never model. **An order the system cannot track is an order the
system should not generate.** Either loop closure is built properly or investigations
stay out of scope.

---

### D18 — State the absent-doctor limitation unprompted

**Rejected:** presenting this as a general answer to Indonesian healthcare access.

**Why:** the system requires a doctor to function, so it helps where doctors are
overloaded and does nothing where doctors are absent — and the access gap is worst
exactly where doctors are absent. The human-in-the-loop design that makes it safe is what
caps its reach. **Naming your own ceiling is more convincing than any claim about the
floor**, and a reviewer who finds this before we do reads it as a gap in our thinking.

---

## Open questions I would ask the client in the first meeting

Listed because knowing what you don't know is part of the deliverable.

1. What EMR/SIMRS is deployed, and can it export structured data today?
2. What is the current mix of BPJS-capitated versus cash-pay patients?
3. What is the actual average consultation duration, measured rather than estimated?
4. Are the doctors employees or contractors? This changes the incentive design entirely.
5. Who signs off clinically, and what is the internal governance process?
6. What is the SATUSEHAT integration status, and is there an active compliance deadline?
7. Who is the named medical director accountable for clinical safety of this system?
8. Does the reviewing doctors' professional liability cover decisions made with AI
   assistance — and does their insurer know?
9. What is the incident reporting path when an AI-assisted decision causes harm?
10. What is the staffing reality at nights, weekends, and **Lebaran**? That determines
    the queue SLA, and it is the question most likely to be answered optimistically.
11. What has already been tried, and why did it fail? **The most valuable question in the
    room, and the one most often skipped.**
